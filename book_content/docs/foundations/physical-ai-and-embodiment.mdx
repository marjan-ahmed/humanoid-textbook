## Concept explanation

Physical AI describes AI systems that operate in the world rather than only inside digital interfaces. A chatbot can be wrong and revise an answer. A robot can be wrong and hit a table, drop an object, collide with a person, overheat a motor, or fall. That difference changes the design discipline entirely.

Embodied intelligence means intelligence is not isolated in a model. It is distributed across the body, sensors, controllers, environment, timing, and feedback loops. A humanoid robot does not simply "understand" a room. It estimates depth, tracks obstacles, predicts motion, balances its body, plans reachable paths, and checks whether a task is still safe at every timestep.

The core insight of Physical AI is that the body is not just a container for the brain. The body is part of the intelligence. Sensors provide information that no language model can infer. Actuators impose constraints that no planner can ignore. The environment provides feedback that no simulation can perfectly predict. Physical AI must account for all of these simultaneously.

## Key ideas

## Worked example: "clean the room"

A person says, "clean the room." A software agent may produce a checklist. A robot must ground the task in physical reality:

| Question | Robot interpretation | Physical constraint |
|---|---|---|
| What is a room? | A mapped physical area with boundaries and obstacles | Requires SLAM mapping and boundary detection |
| What counts as clean? | Objects moved to target locations, trash identified, surfaces accessible | Requires object classification and target placement |
| What can be touched? | Only objects the system can classify and manipulate safely | Requires confidence threshold and grasp planning |
| What can go wrong? | Unknown object, blocked path, low confidence, human enters workspace | Requires safety monitoring and recovery behavior |
| How long will it take? | Depends on room size, object count, navigation speed | Requires time estimation and battery awareness |
| What if something fails? | Object too heavy, path blocked, sensor occluded | Requires fallback strategies and human notification |

## Deep dive: Physical AI vs. software-only AI

Software-only AI mostly manipulates information: text, images, embeddings, tool calls, database records, and interface state. Physical AI manipulates state in the world. Every output must pass through sensors, actuators, controllers, and safety constraints.

| Dimension | Software-only AI | Physical AI |
|---|---|---|
| Error handling | Can revise an answer after a mistake | Must prevent damage before a mistake happens |
| Data quality | Works with digital context | Works with noisy, partial, occluded sensor data |
| Latency impact | Latency affects user experience | Latency can affect safety (100ms = 10cm at 1m/s) |
| Failure mode | Failure can be a message or rollback | Failure may require stopping motion, emergency stop |
| State management | Stateless or simple session state | Continuous state estimation with uncertainty |
| Testing | Unit tests, integration tests | Simulation, hardware-in-the-loop, field testing |
| Cost of failure | Lost time, bad UX | Hardware damage, safety risk, environment impact |

## Deep dive: the reality gap

The reality gap is the mismatch between the robot's internal model and the world itself. A table may look clear in the map, but a transparent cup, moving person, reflective surface, or lighting change can break that assumption. Physical AI systems must constantly check whether their state estimate still matches the environment.

**Common reality gap sources:**

1. **Sensor noise**: Real sensors produce noisy data. Camera images have compression artifacts, LiDAR has angular resolution limits, IMUs have drift.
2. **Calibration error**: Sensors may shift slightly from their calibrated positions. A 2-degree camera tilt can cause 10cm position error at 3m distance.
3. **Lighting variation**: Computer vision models trained on bright scenes may fail in dim rooms, strong shadows, or backlight conditions.
4. **Physics mismatch**: Simulated friction, mass, and contact may not match real materials. A simulated "grasp" may succeed where a real grasp fails due to surface friction.
5. **Dynamic environments**: People move, objects shift, doors open and close. The map from 5 minutes ago may be outdated.
6. **Occlusion**: The robot cannot see everything at once. Objects behind other objects, around corners, or above the camera's field of view are invisible.

## Deep dive: humanoid-specific constraints

Humanoids fit human spaces, but the shape creates difficult constraints:

- **Balance**: A biped must control center of mass while moving. Unlike wheeled robots, humanoids are dynamically unstable — they fall if they stop walking without proper stance control.
- **Reachability**: Arms can only grasp objects inside reachable workspace. The workspace depends on joint limits, body posture, and obstacles.
- **Field of view**: Cameras do not see everything at once. A humanoid must actively look around by rotating its head or body.
- **Contact uncertainty**: Grasping depends on friction, shape, force, material, and surface condition. A firm grasp in simulation may slip in reality.
- **Social safety**: Human-centered spaces include unpredictable human motion. The robot must maintain safe distances, predict human trajectories, and communicate intent.
- **Energy constraints**: Batteries limit operation time. High-compute tasks (perception, planning) consume more power than basic locomotion.
- **Degrees of freedom**: A humanoid may have 30+ joints, each requiring coordinated control. Small errors compound: a 1-degree foot error becomes a 5cm hand position error at arm's length.

## Deep dive: sensor taxonomy

| Sensor type | What it measures | Typical use | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| RGB Camera | Color images | Object detection, scene understanding | Sensitive to lighting, no depth information |
| Depth Camera (RealSense D435i) | Per-pixel distance | 3D mapping, obstacle detection | Limited range (~10m), affected by sunlight |
| LiDAR | Laser distance measurements | Mapping, localization, obstacle detection | Expensive, heavy, affected by rain/dust |
| IMU (BNO055) | Acceleration, angular velocity | Balance, motion estimation | Drift over time, needs calibration |
| Force/Torque Sensor | Contact forces | Grasp verification, collision detection | Limited to contact points |
| Joint Encoder | Joint angle | Proprioception, kinematics | Only measures angle, not force |
| Microphone | Audio | Voice commands, sound detection | Background noise, speaker identification |
| Ultrasonic | Distance via sound | Close-range obstacle detection | Low resolution, affected by temperature |

## Deep dive: the sense-model-plan-act cycle in detail

### 1. Sense (Perception Layer)

The perception layer collects raw sensor data and converts it into structured information. This includes:

- **Camera processing**: Object detection (YOLO, RT-DETR), semantic segmentation, depth estimation
- **LiDAR processing**: Point cloud generation, ground plane detection, obstacle clustering
- **IMU processing**: Orientation estimation (complementary filter, Kalman filter), drift correction
- **Sensor fusion**: Combining multiple sensors to reduce uncertainty (Extended Kalman Filter, particle filters)

### 2. Model (State Estimation Layer)

The modeling layer maintains a consistent estimate of the robot's state and environment:

- **Self-localization**: Where am I? (AMCL, VSLAM, visual odometry)
- **Mapping**: What does the world look like? (occupancy grids, octomaps, feature maps)
- **Object recognition**: What objects exist and where? (6-DoF pose estimation)
- **Affordance analysis**: What can I do here? (reachability, graspability, traversability)

### 3. Plan (Decision Layer)

The planning layer converts goals into actionable steps:

- **Task planning**: Breaking high-level goals into subtasks (Behavior Trees, PDDL)
- **Motion planning**: Finding collision-free paths (RRT*, A*, PRM)
- **Trajectory planning**: Generating smooth joint trajectories (OMPL, CHOMP)
- **Constraint checking**: Ensuring plans satisfy safety and physical limits

### 4. Act (Execution Layer)

The execution layer sends commands to actuators:

- **Motor control**: Converting trajectory points to joint torques (PID, impedance control)
- **Gripper control**: Open/close with force limiting
- **Locomotion**: Bipedal gait generation and balance maintenance
- **Speech output**: Communicating status to humans

### 5. Verify (Feedback Layer)

The verification layer checks whether actions achieved their intended effect:

- **Visual verification**: Did the object move as expected?
- **Proprioceptive verification**: Did the joint reach the target angle?
- **Force verification**: Is the grasp holding?
- **Safety monitoring**: Are all constraints still satisfied?

If verification fails, the system triggers recovery behavior: replan, retry with different parameters, ask for help, or emergency stop.

## Design checklist for embodied tasks

1. What state must the robot estimate? (pose, objects, map, task progress)
2. Which sensors provide that state? (camera, LiDAR, IMU, encoders)
3. What confidence threshold is required before action? (detection confidence, localization accuracy)
4. What constraints limit the action? (speed, force, reachability, collision, battery)
5. What feedback confirms success? (visual, proprioceptive, force)
6. What recovery path handles failure? (replan, retry, ask, stop)
7. What is the maximum acceptable latency? (safety-critical vs. best-effort)
8. What happens if a sensor fails? (degraded mode, emergency stop)

## Quiz

### Check your understanding

1. Why is Physical AI harder than text-only AI?
1. What is the difference between planning and acting?
1. Name two sensors that help a robot understand physical space.
1. Why should simulation come before physical deployment?
1. What is the reality gap and why does it matter?
1. Explain why a 100ms latency is dangerous for a walking humanoid.
1. What is an affordance and how does it differ from a geometric property?

### Answer key

1. Physical AI must handle safety, timing, sensors, uncertainty, and real-world consequences that software-only AI does not face.
1. Planning proposes steps; acting executes them through controllers under physical constraints. Planning can be revised; acting has physical consequences.
1. Examples: depth camera (RealSense D435i), LiDAR, RGB camera, IMU (BNO055), force/torque sensor.
1. Simulation exposes failures without risking people, hardware, or the environment. It allows rapid iteration and testing of edge cases.
1. The reality gap is the mismatch between simulated and real-world conditions. It matters because behaviors that work in simulation may fail in reality due to noise, friction differences, lighting, and calibration errors.
1. At 1m/s, 100ms latency means the robot moves 10cm before it can react. For a humanoid, this can cause a fall or collision.
1. An affordance is what the world allows the robot to do (a handle can be pulled), while a geometric property is just the shape (a cylinder has radius 2cm). Affordances depend on the robot's capabilities, not just the object's form.

## Glossary

- **Physical AI**: AI systems that operate in the physical world through sensors, actuators, and controllers, as opposed to software-only AI.
- **Embodiment**: Intelligence distributed across body, sensors, controllers, environment, and feedback loops.
- **State Estimation**: The process of inferring the robot's current condition from sensor data.
- **Affordance**: What the world allows the robot to do, determined by the robot's capabilities and the object's properties.
- **Reality Gap**: The mismatch between simulated conditions and real-world behavior.
- **Latency**: The delay between sensing and acting, measured in milliseconds.
- **Uncertainty**: The gap between what the robot believes and what is actually true.
- **Feedback Loop**: The cycle of sensing, reasoning, acting, and verifying that drives embodied intelligence.
- **Recovery Behavior**: Automated responses to failures, including replanning, retrying, asking for help, or emergency stopping.
