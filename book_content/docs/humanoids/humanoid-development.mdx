## Concept explanation

### Why humanoid form factors matter

Humanoid robots are engineered for environments built around people. Doors, staircases, tables, tool handles, shelves, and kitchen appliances all assume a human-like body plan: an upright torso, two articulated arms with hands, two legs with ankle and hip joints, and a head carrying cameras and microphones. That morphological alignment means a humanoid can in principle use the same tools, navigate the same pathways, and interact with the same interfaces as a human operator. This is the central thesis of the humanoid form factor: embodiment in human spaces reduces the need for environmental modification.

However, this morphological advantage comes at enormous control cost. A humanoid with 28 degrees of freedom (DOF) must coordinate 28 actuators simultaneously while maintaining dynamic balance on two feet with a small support polygon. Small errors compound nonlinearly: a 5-millimeter foot placement error shifts the center of mass, which destabilizes the torso, which forces the arms to swing for counterbalance, which disrupts any ongoing manipulation task. The coupling between subsystems makes humanoid robotics one of the most demanding problems in applied control theory.

### The control stack

A humanoid control stack is organized in layers, each operating at a different frequency and abstraction level:

1. **Joint-level torque control** (1 kHz): Each actuator receives a torque command based on a PID or impedance controller. This layer handles low-level safety limits, current saturation, and backlash compensation.
2. **Whole-body control** (500 Hz): A quadratic program or operational-space controller distributes desired end-effector forces and torques across all joints while respecting friction cones, torque limits, and balance constraints.
3. **Motion planning** (50-100 Hz): A planner generates feasible trajectories for footsteps, arm reaches, and torso reorientation. It uses preview control or model predictive control (MPC) to anticipate future steps.
4. **Perception** (10-30 Hz): Depth cameras, lidar, and tactile sensors feed a state estimator that localizes the robot, identifies obstacles, and tracks humans in the scene.
5. **Task-level reasoning** (1-10 Hz): A high-level planner or learned policy selects tasks, allocates resources, and handles failures and retries.

### Key engineering challenges

| Challenge | Why it is hard | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Dynamic balance | The support polygon is small; the robot is always partially falling | Fall, hardware damage |
| Contact transitions | Foot-ground contact is hybrid (discrete + continuous) | Stumble, slip |
| Whole-body coordination | 20+ DOF are coupled through balance and momentum | Task failure, oscillation |
| Perception under motion | Camera images blur and shift as the body moves | Localization error |
| Battery life | High-power actuators drain batteries in minutes | Mission timeout |
| Payload vs. weight | More payload requires larger actuators, which add weight | Reduced mobility |
| Safety around humans | Rigid links and high-torque actuators can injure people | Injury, liability |

## Deep dive subtopics

### Forward and inverse kinematics

#### Forward kinematics

Forward kinematics (FK) computes the position and orientation of each link given a vector of joint angles. For a serial chain of $n$ joints, the FK is expressed using homogeneous transformation matrices:

$$
T_i^\{i-1\} = \begin\{bmatrix\}
R_i(\theta_i) & d_i \\
0 & 1
\end\{bmatrix\}
$$

where $R_i(\theta_i)$ is the $3 \times 3$ rotation matrix for joint $i$ (determined by the Denavit-Hartenberg parameters $\alpha_i, a_i, d_i, \theta_i$) and $d_i$ is the translation vector. The pose of the end-effector relative to the base is:

$$
T_n^0 = T_1^0 \cdot T_2^1 \cdot \ldots \cdot T_n^\{n-1\} = \prod_\{i=1\}^\{n\} T_i^\{i-1\}
$$

The position of the end-effector is extracted from the last column of $T_n^0$, and its orientation from the upper-left $3 \times 3$ submatrix.

#### Inverse kinematics

Inverse kinematics (IK) answers the harder question: given a desired end-effector pose $T_d$, find the joint vector $\mathbf\{q\}$ such that $FK(\mathbf\{q\}) = T_d$. For redundant manipulators (more DOF than the task requires), IK is underdetermined and requires additional optimization criteria (e.g., minimize joint torque, avoid singularities, maximize manipulability).

The Jacobian-based IK solution uses the geometric Jacobian $J(\mathbf\{q\})$:

$$
\dot\{\mathbf\{x\}\} = J(\mathbf\{q\}) \dot\{\mathbf\{q\}\}
$$

A damped least-squares IK step is:

$$
\Delta \mathbf\{q\} = J^T (J J^T + \lambda^2 I)^\{-1\} \Delta \mathbf\{x\}
$$

where $\Delta \mathbf\{x\}$ is the pose error and $\lambda$ is the damping factor that prevents ill-conditioning near singularities. In practice, IK is solved at every control cycle (50-500 Hz) to track time-varying trajectories.

#### Kinematics for humanoid legs

For a humanoid leg with 6 DOF (3 at the hip, 1 at the knee, 2 at the ankle), IK computes the foot pose relative to the hip given joint angles, or conversely computes joint angles to place the foot at a desired location. The leg IK is critical for:

- **Footstep placement**: Computing hip and knee angles to place the foot at a target position during swing phase.
- **Balance adjustment**: Computing ankle angles to tilt the torso relative to the ground.
- **Terrain adaptation**: Adjusting foot orientation to match slope or stair height.

### Center of mass and balance

#### Center of mass calculation

The center of mass (CoM) of a humanoid is the mass-weighted average position of all links:

$$
\mathbf\{r\}_\{CoM\} = \frac\{\sum_\{i=1\}^\{N\} m_i \mathbf\{r\}_i\}\{\sum_\{i=1\}^\{N\} m_i\}
$$

where $m_i$ is the mass of link $i$ and $\mathbf\{r\}_i$ is the position of link $i$'s center of mass in the world frame. For a humanoid with 20 links, this requires tracking the FK of every link and summing their contributions.

The CoM velocity is:

$$
\dot\{\mathbf\{r\}\}_\{CoM\} = \frac\{\sum_\{i=1\}^\{N\} m_i \dot\{\mathbf\{r\}\}_i\}\{\sum_\{i=1\}^\{N\} m_i\}
$$

and the CoM acceleration is:

$$
\ddot\{\mathbf\{r\}\}_\{CoM\} = \frac\{\sum_\{i=1\}^\{N\} m_i \ddot\{\mathbf\{r\}\}_i\}\{\sum_\{i=1\}^\{N\} m_i\}
$$

For balance control, the CoM projection onto the ground plane must remain within or near the support polygon (the convex hull of foot contact points).

#### Zero moment point (ZMP)

The ZMP is the point on the ground where the total moment of ground reaction forces about the horizontal axes is zero. The ZMP condition for balance is:

$$
\mathbf\{p\}_\{ZMP\} = \mathbf\{r\}_\{CoM\} - \frac\{\ddot\{\mathbf\{r\}\}_\{CoM\} \cdot z_\{CoM\}\}\{g + \ddot\{z\}_\{CoM\}\}
$$

where $z_\{CoM\}$ is the CoM height and $g$ is gravitational acceleration. If $\mathbf\{p\}_\{ZMP\}$ lies within the support polygon, the robot is statically stable. If it lies outside, the robot will tip.

#### Capture point

The capture point (CP) extends ZMP analysis to dynamic walking. It is the point on the ground where the robot would need to place its next footstep to come to a complete stop:

$$
\mathbf\{r\}_\{CP\} = \mathbf\{r\}_\{CoM\} + \frac\{\dot\{\mathbf\{r\}\}_\{CoM\}\}\{\omega_0\}
$$

where $\omega_0 = \sqrt\{g / z_\{CoM\}\}$ is the natural frequency of the linear inverted pendulum model. If the capture point is within the support polygon, the robot can stop. If it is outside, the robot cannot stop without falling or taking an additional step.

#### Linear inverted pendulum model (LIPM)

The LIPM is the simplest dynamic model for bipedal walking. It assumes the CoM moves at constant height $z_\{CoM\}$, and the horizontal dynamics decouple:

$$
\ddot\{x\} = \frac\{g\}\{z_\{CoM\}\} (x - p_x)
$$
$$
\ddot\{y\} = \frac\{g\}\{z_\{CoM\}\} (y - p_y)
$$

where $(p_x, p_y)$ is the ZMP location. This linear model enables preview control and MPC for gait planning.

### Gait patterns

#### Walking gait

A bipedal walking gait consists of alternating stance and swing phases:

1. **Double support**: Both feet on the ground. The CoM transfers from one foot to the other. Duration: 10-20% of gait cycle.
2. **Single support (stance)**: One foot is planted. The CoM arcs over the stance foot. Duration: 40-50% of gait cycle.
3. **Swing**: The free leg swings forward. The knee flexes to clear the ground. Duration: 30-40% of gait cycle.

The walking pattern generator computes a CoM trajectory (typically a sinusoidal or cubic spline in the lateral and sagittal planes) and footstep locations, then uses the LIPM or preview control to generate joint trajectories.

#### Running gait

Running introduces a flight phase (both feet off the ground) and requires higher actuator power. The key differences from walking:

- **Flight phase**: 20-40% of gait cycle. The CoM follows a ballistic parabola.
- **Higher impact forces**: Landing forces can reach 2-3x body weight.
- **Elastic energy storage**: Tendons or springs store and release energy during push-off.
- **Shorter ground contact time**: Requires faster actuator response.

Running gaits are typically generated using spring-mass models or centroidal momentum dynamics.

#### Stair climbing

Stair climbing requires:
- **Precise foot placement**: Each step must land on a tread with sufficient clearance.
- **High knee lift**: The swing leg must clear the riser height.
- **Vertical CoM displacement**: The CoM must rise by the riser height each step.
- **Higher joint torques**: Hip and knee torques increase significantly due to lifting the body weight against gravity.

Stair climbing is often planned using a stepped CoM trajectory and modified footstep planner that accounts for discrete height changes.

#### Terrain adaptation

Uneven terrain requires:
- **Foot orientation control**: The ankle must conform to ground slope.
- **Variable step height**: The swing leg trajectory must adapt to obstacles.
- **Real-time perception**: A depth camera or force sensor detects terrain geometry ahead of the robot.
- **Reactive replanning**: If foot contact is unexpected (e.g., slipping), the planner must immediately generate a recovery step.

### Force and torque control for manipulation

#### Impedance control

Impedance control regulates the relationship between end-effector position error and interaction force:

$$
\mathbf\{F\} = K (\mathbf\{x\}_d - \mathbf\{x\}) + D (\dot\{\mathbf\{x\}\}_d - \dot\{\mathbf\{x\}\})
$$

where $K$ is the stiffness matrix and $D$ is the damping matrix. A compliant humanoid arm with low $K$ can safely contact objects without excessive force, while a stiff arm with high $K$ achieves precise positioning.

#### Grasp planning

Grasp planning for humanoid hands involves:

1. **Pre-grasp configuration**: Move the hand to a position above the object with fingers open.
2. **Approach trajectory**: Move along the approach vector (typically normal to the grasp surface).
3. **Finger closure**: Close fingers until contact is detected via tactile sensors or motor current.
4. **Force regulation**: Apply sufficient grip force to prevent slip without crushing the object.
5. **Lift verification**: Confirm the object is held by monitoring force/torque at the wrist.

The grasp quality is evaluated using metrics such as the grasp wrench space (GWS) or the epsilon quality measure, which quantifies the maximum disturbance wrench the grasp can resist.

#### Contact sequencing

For multi-contact manipulation (e.g., bracing against a table while reaching), the robot must sequence contacts to maintain balance while achieving the task. The contact planner selects:

- Which links contact the environment
- The order of contact establishment and release
- The forces applied at each contact

### Social robotics and proxemics

#### Proxemics zones

Proxemics defines four spatial zones for human-human interaction, which apply directly to human-robot interaction:

| Zone | Distance | Use case for robots |
|---|---|---|
| Intimate | 0-0.45 m | Medical robots, assistive care |
| Personal | 0.45-1.2 m | Collaborative manipulation, handover |
| Social | 1.2-3.6 m | Service robots, reception, greeting |
| Public | >3.6 m | Warehouse, patrol, delivery |

#### Legibility and predictability

A socially competent robot communicates its intent so that humans can predict what it will do next:

- **Legibility**: The robot's motion should be readable by the human observer. For example, a reaching motion should have a clear, direct trajectory rather than an ambiguous curved path.
- **Predictability**: The robot should behave consistently in repeated situations so the human can learn to anticipate its actions.
- **Velocity profiling**: Accelerating toward the human may feel aggressive; a bell-shaped velocity profile feels more natural.

#### gaze and gesture

- **Gaze direction**: Looking at the object before reaching signals intent. Looking at the human signals attention or request for confirmation.
- **Head orientation**: Turning the head toward the next motion direction before the body moves improves legibility.
- **Gesture**: A pointing gesture or a nod can communicate intent without speech.
- **Personal space respect**: The robot should pause or slow down when entering the personal zone, giving the human time to react.

### Balance control algorithm

The following pseudocode describes a real-time balance controller that computes ankle and hip torques to maintain the CoM above the support polygon during single-support phase.

```python
import numpy as np

class BalanceController:
    """
    Real-time balance controller for bipedal humanoid.
    Uses ZMP-based feedback with capture point regulation.
    """

    def __init__(self, mass, com_height, gravity=9.81):
        self.mass = mass
        self.com_height = com_height
        self.gravity = gravity
        self.omega_0 = np.sqrt(gravity / com_height)

        # Feedback gains
        self.kp_zmp = 500.0    # ZMP error proportional gain
        self.kd_zmp = 100.0    # ZMP error derivative gain
        self.kp_capture = 2.0  # Capture point gain
        self.kd_capture = 0.5  # Capture point damping

        # State limits
        self.max_ankle_torque = 30.0  # Nm
        self.max_hip_torque = 40.0    # Nm

    def compute_com_state(self, joint_positions, joint_velocities, mass_properties):
        """Compute CoM position and velocity from joint states."""
        com_pos = np.zeros(3)
        com_vel = np.zeros(3)
        total_mass = 0.0

        for i, link in enumerate(mass_properties):
            m = link['mass']
            r = link['com_offset']  # offset from joint
            q = joint_positions[i]
            qd = joint_velocities[i]

            # Simplified: compute link COM in world frame
            com_pos += m * (link['joint_pos'] + rotate(q, r))
            com_vel += m * link['joint_vel']
            total_mass += m

        com_pos /= total_mass
        com_vel /= total_mass
        return com_pos, com_vel

    def compute_zmp(self, com_pos, com_vel, com_accel):
        """Compute zero moment point from CoM state."""
        x_com, y_com = com_pos[0], com_pos[1]
        x_ddot, y_ddot = com_accel[0], com_accel[1]
        z_com = self.com_height

        # ZMP position (sagittal and lateral)
        p_zmp_x = x_com - (x_ddot * z_com) / (self.gravity + 0.0)
        p_zmp_y = y_com - (y_ddot * z_com) / (self.gravity + 0.0)

        return np.array([p_zmp_x, p_zmp_y])

    def compute_capture_point(self, com_pos, com_vel):
        """Compute capture point for dynamic stopping."""
        return com_pos[:2] + com_vel[:2] / self.omega_0

    def compute_support_polygon(self, foot_positions):
        """Compute convex hull of foot contact points."""
        # Simplified: use foot corners
        points = []
        for foot in foot_positions:
            center = foot['position']
            half_l = foot['length'] / 2
            half_w = foot['width'] / 2
            R = rotation_matrix(foot['orientation'])
            corners = [
                R @ np.array([half_l, half_w, 0]),
                R @ np.array([half_l, -half_w, 0]),
                R @ np.array([-half_l, half_w, 0]),
                R @ np.array([-half_l, -half_w, 0]),
            ]
            points.extend([center + c[:2] for c in corners])
        return np.array(points)

    def point_in_polygon(self, point, polygon):
        """Check if point is inside convex polygon using cross products."""
        n = len(polygon)
        inside = True
        for i in range(n):
            edge = polygon[(i + 1) % n] - polygon[i]
            to_point = point - polygon[i]
            cross = edge[0] * to_point[1] - edge[1] * to_point[0]
            if cross < 0:
                inside = False
                break
        return inside

    def compute_balance_torques(self, com_pos, com_vel, com_accel,
                                 foot_positions, stance_foot_idx):
        """
        Compute ankle and hip torques to regulate balance.

        Returns:
            ankle_torque: Torque to apply at the stance ankle
            hip_torque: Torque to apply at the hip for balance
        """
        # Compute current ZMP
        zmp = self.compute_zmp(com_pos, com_vel, com_accel)

        # Compute support polygon
        support_poly = self.compute_support_polygon(foot_positions)

        # Compute capture point
        cp = self.compute_capture_point(com_pos, com_vel)

        # Desired ZMP (center of support polygon)
        zmp_desired = np.mean(support_poly, axis=0)

        # ZMP error
        zmp_error = zmp - zmp_desired
        zmp_error_dot = -self.kp_zmp * zmp_error  # simplified derivative

        # Ankle torque (sagittal plane)
        ankle_torque = (
            self.kp_zmp * zmp_error[1] +
            self.kd_zmp * zmp_error_dot[1]
        )

        # Hip torque (lateral balance, from capture point regulation)
        cp_desired = zmp_desired  # want CP near center of support
        cp_error = cp - cp_desired
        hip_torque = (
            self.kp_capture * cp_error[1] +
            self.kd_capture * (-com_vel[1])
        )

        # Clamp to limits
        ankle_torque = np.clip(ankle_torque,
                                -self.max_ankle_torque,
                                self.max_ankle_torque)
        hip_torque = np.clip(hip_torque,
                              -self.max_hip_torque,
                              self.max_hip_torque)

        return ankle_torque, hip_torque

    def step(self, dt, com_pos, com_vel, com_accel,
             foot_positions, stance_foot_idx):
        """
        Single control step. Call at 500 Hz.

        Returns:
            torque_commands: dict of joint torques
        """
        ankle_torque, hip_torque = self.compute_balance_torques(
            com_pos, com_vel, com_accel,
            foot_positions, stance_foot_idx
        )

        torque_commands = {
            'left_ankle': ankle_torque if stance_foot_idx == 0 else 0.0,
            'right_ankle': ankle_torque if stance_foot_idx == 1 else 0.0,
            'left_hip_roll': hip_torque if stance_foot_idx == 0 else 0.0,
            'right_hip_roll': hip_torque if stance_foot_idx == 1 else 0.0,
        }

        return torque_commands
```

### Grasp planning with inverse kinematics

The following example demonstrates a grasp planning pipeline that uses IK to compute joint configurations for reaching and grasping an object with a humanoid arm.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

class GraspPlanner:
    """
    Grasp planner for a 7-DOF humanoid arm.
    Computes IK solutions for pre-grasp, grasp, and lift poses.
    """

    def __init__(self, dh_params):
        """
        Args:
            dh_params: List of (a, alpha, d, theta_offset) for each joint
        """
        self.dh = dh_params
        self.n_joints = len(dh_params)

        # Grasp parameters
        self.approach_distance = 0.15  # meters above object
        self.grip_force = 10.0  # Newtons
        self.lift_height = 0.05  # meters

    def forward_kinematics(self, q):
        """Compute end-effector pose from joint angles."""
        T = np.eye(4)
        for i in range(self.n_joints):
            a, alpha, d, theta_off = self.dh[i]
            theta = q[i] + theta_off

            Ti = np.array([
                [np.cos(theta), -np.sin(theta)*np.cos(alpha),
                 np.sin(theta)*np.sin(alpha), a*np.cos(theta)],
                [np.sin(theta), np.cos(theta)*np.cos(alpha),
                 -np.cos(theta)*np.sin(alpha), a*np.sin(theta)],
                [0, np.sin(alpha), np.cos(alpha), d],
                [0, 0, 0, 1]
            ])
            T = T @ Ti

        position = T[:3, 3]
        rotation = T[:3, :3]
        return position, rotation

    def jacobian(self, q, delta=1e-6):
        """Numerical Jacobian for position control."""
        J = np.zeros((3, self.n_joints))
        pos0, _ = self.forward_kinematics(q)

        for i in range(self.n_joints):
            q_plus = q.copy()
            q_plus[i] += delta
            pos_plus, _ = self.forward_kinematics(q_plus)
            J[:, i] = (pos_plus - pos0) / delta

        return J

    def solve_ik(self, target_pos, q_init=None, max_iter=100, tol=1e-4):
        """
        Solve IK using damped least-squares.

        Args:
            target_pos: Desired end-effector position (3,)
            q_init: Initial joint configuration
            max_iter: Maximum iterations
            tol: Position error tolerance

        Returns:
            q_solution: Joint angles achieving target position
            success: Whether IK converged
        """
        if q_init is None:
            q_init = np.zeros(self.n_joints)

        q = q_init.copy()

        for iteration in range(max_iter):
            pos, _ = self.forward_kinematics(q)
            error = target_pos - pos

            if np.linalg.norm(error) < tol:
                return q, True

            J = self.jacobian(q)
            # Damped least-squares
            lambda_d = 0.1
            dq = J.T @ np.linalg.solve(
                J @ J.T + lambda_d**2 * np.eye(3), error
            )

            # Joint limit clamping
            q = np.clip(q + dq, -np.pi, np.pi)

        return q, False

    def plan_grasp(self, object_pos, object_orientation, hand_width):
        """
        Plan a complete grasp sequence.

        Args:
            object_pos: Object center position (3,)
            object_orientation: Object orientation as rotation matrix (3,3)
            hand_width: Width of the object for grip aperture

        Returns:
            Waypoints: List of (position, grip_width, phase) tuples
        """
        waypoints = []

        # Phase 1: Pre-grasp (above and behind the object)
        approach_dir = object_orientation[:, 2]  # approach along z-axis
        pre_grasp_pos = object_pos + self.approach_distance * approach_dir
        pre_grasp_pos[2] += 0.05  # slight lift for clearance
        waypoints.append((pre_grasp_pos, hand_width * 1.2, 'approach'))

        # Phase 2: Approach (move down to grasp height)
        grasp_pos = object_pos.copy()
        waypoints.append((grasp_pos, hand_width * 1.1, 'approach'))

        # Phase 3: Grasp (close fingers)
        waypoints.append((grasp_pos, hand_width * 0.9, 'grasp'))

        # Phase 4: Lift
        lift_pos = grasp_pos.copy()
        lift_pos[2] += self.lift_height
        waypoints.append((lift_pos, hand_width * 0.9, 'lift'))

        return waypoints

    def compute_grasp_quality(self, contact_points, contact_normals,
                               friction_coeff=0.5):
        """
        Compute epsilon quality measure for a grasp.

        Args:
            contact_points: List of contact positions (3, n_contacts)
            contact_normals: List of contact normals (3, n_contacts)
            friction_coeff: Coulomb friction coefficient

        Returns:
            quality: Scalar grasp quality (higher is better)
        """
        n_contacts = contact_points.shape[1]

        # Build grasp matrix (maps contact wrenches to object wrench)
        G = np.zeros((6, 6 * n_contacts))

        for i in range(n_contacts):
            r = contact_points[:, i]
            n = contact_normals[:, i]

            # Contact Jacobian (force component)
            G[:3, 6*i:6*i+3] = np.eye(3)
            # Contact Jacobian (moment component)
            G[3:, 6*i:6*i+3] = skew_symmetric(r)
            # Friction cone constraint (simplified)
            G[3:, 6*i+3:6*i+6] = np.eye(3) * friction_coeff

        # Compute singular values
        _, s, _ = np.linalg.svd(G)

        # Epsilon quality: minimum singular value
        quality = s[-1]

        return quality


def skew_symmetric(v):
    """Compute skew-symmetric matrix from 3-vector."""
    return np.array([
        [0, -v[2], v[1]],
        [v[2], 0, -v[0]],
        [-v[1], v[0], 0]
    ])
```

## Quiz

### Check your understanding

1. Why are humanoids harder to control than wheeled robots?
2. What is the zero moment point, and why does it matter for balance?
3. How does the capture point differ from the ZMP?
4. What is the primary advantage of impedance control over position control for manipulation?
5. What are the four proxemics zones, and which zone applies to a robot handing a cup to a person?
6. Name two reasons why a full humanoid costs more than a quadruped with the same compute.
7. Why does bipedal stair climbing require higher joint torques than flat-ground walking?
8. What is the damped least-squares method, and why is it used in IK?
9. How does the linear inverted pendulum model simplify bipedal gait planning?
10. In a staged lab strategy, why should you add only one new risk surface at a time?

### Answer key

1. They must manage dynamic balance on a small support polygon, coordinate 20+ coupled joints, handle hybrid contact transitions, and perform manipulation simultaneously. Wheeled robots have a stable base and far fewer DOF.
2. The ZMP is the point on the ground where the total moment of ground reaction forces about the horizontal axes is zero. If the ZMP lies within the support polygon, the robot is statically stable. If it lies outside, the robot will tip.
3. The ZMP indicates current stability; the capture point indicates where the robot must place its next footstep to stop. The capture point extends ZMP analysis to dynamic, multi-step walking.
4. Impedance control regulates the relationship between position error and interaction force, allowing compliant contact with objects. Position control blindly tries to reach a target regardless of contact forces, which can damage the object or robot.
5. Intimate (0-0.45 m), personal (0.45-1.2 m), social (1.2-3.6 m), public (>3.6 m). Handing a cup occurs in the personal zone.
6. Humanoids require bipedal legs with 6 DOF each (for balance and terrain adaptation), a heavier torso for upright posture, and more complex whole-body controllers. Quadrupeds have a naturally stable four-point base.
7. The CoM must rise by the riser height each step, requiring sustained hip and knee extension against gravity. Additionally, the swing leg must lift high enough to clear the riser, adding to the torque demand.
8. It computes IK solutions as $\Delta \mathbf\{q\} = J^T (J J^T + \lambda^2 I)^\{-1\} \Delta \mathbf\{x\}$, which avoids ill-conditioning near singularities by adding a damping term $\lambda^2 I$ to the matrix being inverted.
9. The LIPM assumes constant CoM height and decouples horizontal dynamics into two independent linear equations, enabling preview control and MPC for efficient gait generation.
10. Each new subsystem (sensors, actuators, communication, autonomy) introduces potential failure modes. Adding one at a time allows isolation and debugging without overwhelming the team with simultaneous unknowns.

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Bipedal locomotion** | Walking on two legs, requiring continuous dynamic balance management |
| **Capture point (CP)** | The point on the ground where a footstep would bring the robot to a complete stop |
| **Center of mass (CoM)** | The mass-weighted average position of all links in a robot |
| **Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) parameters** | Four parameters (a, alpha, d, theta) that describe the geometry of each joint-link pair |
| **Degrees of freedom (DOF)** | The number of independent variables needed to fully describe a robot's configuration |
| **Dynamixel servo** | A line of smart actuators commonly used in educational and research robots |
| **Epsilon quality** | A grasp quality metric based on the minimum singular value of the grasp matrix |
| **Forward kinematics (FK)** | Computing end-effector position and orientation from joint angles |
| **Gait cycle** | One complete sequence of stance and swing phases for all legs |
| **Grasp wrench space (GWS)** | The set of all wrenches a grasp can resist |
| **Homogeneous transformation** | A 4x4 matrix representing rotation and translation in a single operation |
| **Impedance control** | A control strategy that regulates the dynamic relationship between position error and interaction force |
| **Inverse kinematics (IK)** | Computing joint angles to achieve a desired end-effector pose |
| **Jacobian** | A matrix relating joint velocities to end-effector velocities |
| **Legibility** | The degree to which a robot's motion can be read and understood by a human observer |
| **Linear inverted pendulum model (LIPM)** | A simplified dynamic model assuming constant CoM height for bipedal walking analysis |
| **Model predictive control (MPC)** | An optimization-based control method that predicts future states and optimizes actions over a finite horizon |
| **Operational-space control** | Controlling end-effector forces and torques directly, rather than individual joint torques |
| **Proxemics** | The study of human use of space and the effects of personal distance on interaction |
| **Quadratic program (QP)** | An optimization problem with a quadratic objective and linear constraints, used in whole-body control |
| **SLAM** | Simultaneous Localization and Mapping: building a map while localizing the robot within it |
| **Spring-mass model** | A simplified running model treating the leg as a spring and the body as a point mass |
| **Support polygon** | The convex hull of all ground contact points; the CoM projection must stay within for static stability |
| **URDF** | Unified Robot Description Format: an XML standard for describing robot kinematics, dynamics, and visual appearance |
| **Whole-body control** | Coordinating all joints simultaneously to achieve desired end-effector forces while maintaining balance |
| **Zero moment point (ZMP)** | The point on the ground where the total moment of ground reaction forces about horizontal axes is zero |
